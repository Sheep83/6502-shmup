# Level 2 PCB Terrain — Visual Prototype

An experimental Level 2 terrain vocabulary that makes the stage a giant printed
circuit board. It is a **construction kit**, not a painting: the trace pieces are
built around a fixed edge contract, and both the contract and the showcase map's
conformance to it are checked by machine rather than by eye.

Nothing in the shipped game imports it. Canonical Level 1 is byte-for-byte
unchanged and is proven so below.

Read out of current source, not from any previous report.

| Constraint | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Terrain glyph codes | `96 .. 223` — **128 slots** | `engine_data.py:41-42` |
| Glyph count on export | must be a **multiple of 8** | `export_v6.py:378` |
| Turret glyphs | base **226** — must not collide | `contract_v2.py` |
| Metatile definitions | max **64**, each exactly **16** char codes | `contract_v2.py` |
| Metatile geometry | 4×4 chars = **16 MC px × 32 rows** = 32×32 *screen* px | `native_metatile.py:12-13` |
| Map width | **10** metatiles, fixed | `contract_v2.py` |
| Map height | **7 .. 440** metatile rows | `contract_v2.py` |
| Colour model | every cell multicolour; `0→$d021 1→$d022 2→$d023 3→colour RAM` | `native_metatile.py:6-8` |
| Colour RAM | **written once at init, never again** | `src/terrain.asm` |
| Character colour | **0..7 only** (bit 3 forces multicolour) | `validation_v6.py:187` |
| bg / mc1 / mc2 | 0..15 | `validation_v6.py:182` |

Two of these shaped every decision that follows.

**There are four colours for the entire level.** Colour RAM is written once at
init and is not double buffered — there is one `$d800` for both screen pages — so
terrain colour is level-global. No per-cell or per-region colour exists. All
shading must come from four fixed entries.

**A multicolour pixel is two screen pixels wide and one tall.** Every vertical
stroke is therefore half the pixel-width of an equally thick horizontal one, and
anything meant to look round must be twice as tall as it is wide in native
units. This is not cosmetic: it is why traces are 2 px wide vertically and 4 rows
tall horizontally, and it is the reason the substrate cannot be dithered (§10).

### Authoring path

The editor's own representation is the **native 16×32 multicolour grid**
(`levelMetatileSet`), from which `native_metatile.pack_metatiles()` derives the
packed glyphs and metatile definitions with first-seen deduplication. The
prototype authors native grids and uses that packer, so the glyph set is
generated by the editor's own code and the project is editable after the fact.

## 4. Palette

| Logical | Register | C64 colour | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | `$d021` background | **5 green** | bare solder-mask substrate |
| 1 | `$d022` mc1 | **0 black** | IC bodies, drill holes, outlines |
| 2 | `$d023` mc2 | **15 light grey** | traces, solder, silkscreen |
| 3 | colour RAM | **7 yellow** | gold pads, vias, test points, pins |

This is exactly the four roles the brief asked for, and it is the closest useful
combination the renderer permits. Substrate is the *background*, so bare board
costs one all-zero glyph and the quiet parts of the map are free. Character
colour is capped at 0..7 by the engine and yellow is 7, so the gold is reachable.

Level 1's palette is greys (bg 12, mc1 15, mc2 11, char 1), so a green board is
immediately and unmistakably a different place.

**An honest reservation about the green, which is the main visual weakness.**
Sampled out of a real `x64sc` capture, C64 colour 5 is `(98, 213, 50)` — a vivid
*grass* green, not the dark olive of solder mask. Large quiet areas of it read as
a field rather than a board. C64 has exactly two greens and the other (13) is a
pastel, so 5 is the only candidate; the usual remedy of dithering it darker
against black does not work here (§10). Colour **6 blue** reads considerably more
convincingly as a circuit board and costs nothing, so it is provided as a second
palette — but green is the default because green is what was asked for.

```
python3 tools/level_editor/gen_level2_pcb.py --palette=blue
```

Evidence: `reports/level2-pcb-terrain-visual-prototype/palette-study-dithers.png`,
`palette-study-stripes.png`, `vice-blue-endofstage.png`.

## 5. The edge contract

This is the part that makes the set a kit. Connection positions are fixed once,
for the whole vocabulary, and no tile's edges were drawn by eye.

A metatile is 16 native px across and 32 down. Metal may cross an edge **only**
in these lanes:

```
vertical lanes (cross the TOP and BOTTOM edges)
    VL  x  3..4        VC  x  7..8        VR  x 11..12
horizontal lanes (cross the LEFT and RIGHT edges)
    HT  y  6..9        HC  y 14..17       HB  y 22..25
power lanes (deliberately not interoperable with the above)
    VP  x  6..9        HP  y 12..19
```

In **screen** pixels those are the quarter, half and three-quarter points of the
metatile in both axes, and each set is its own mirror image: `x → 15-x` swaps
VL↔VR and fixes VC; `y → 31-y` swaps HT↔HB and fixes HC. That symmetry is why
most of the vocabulary is generated by mirroring one authored tile.

Metatiles tile the screen on exact 4-character boundaries, so a trace leaving one
tile in lane VC meets lane VC of its neighbour with **no possible
misalignment** — alignment is a property of the lane table, not of the artwork.

Lane widths are set by the aspect ratio: a vertical trace is 2 native px (4 screen
px) and a horizontal one is 4 native rows (4 screen px), so the two read as
equally thick. Power rails are double that in both axes.

VC and HC are the primary lanes and carry almost everything. VL/VR exist for
buses; HT/HB for buses and IC pin rows.

**IC pins sit on HT and HB, never HC** — two pins per metatile edge at a 16-row
pitch, which is the same pitch across a tile seam as within a tile, so a chip
body stacks to any height with evenly spaced legs. A horizontal trace arriving on
HC therefore does not land on a pin; `FAN_R`/`FAN_L` split HC into HT+HB for
exactly that reason, and `BUS_H2` carries the pair.

### Two other kinds of edge, and why they are not faults

* **Body seams.** The left half of a DIP meets its right half along a continuous
  black package; a header shroud runs on down the strip; a bank of edge fingers
  is an areal pattern that repeats every four native pixels and so continues
  across a boundary by construction. These carry metal outside any lane.
* **Pin terminals.** A pin may end in bare board — real boards are full of pins
  connected to nothing — whereas a *trace* that stops dead is a hole in the
  artwork.

Both are exempted **by name**, not by weakening the rule for everything. The leg
edges of the very same chip tiles are still lane-checked, and they are the joins
that matter most.

### The contract is enforced by machine

`gen_level2_pcb.py` runs two checks on every invocation:

* `check_lane_discipline()` — no tile may put metal on an edge anywhere but in a
  whole declared lane.
* `check_map_continuity()` — for every adjacent pair in the showcase map, the
  metal crossing the shared edge must match on both sides.

Current output: **0 lane faults, 0 open joins.** The second check earned its
keep — it found thirteen real authoring errors in the first dense map draft
(buses starting out of bare board, horizontal runs passing *through* a net
instead of turning into it, `TRACE_TN` used mid-net when it has no bottom edge),
each reported with its map coordinate.

### How larger components are assembled

A **DIP** is two metatiles wide and any number tall: `DIP_TOP_L|R`, then
`DIP_MID_L|R` repeated, then `DIP_BOT_L|R`. Legs are on HT and HB on the outer
edges at a uniform 16-row pitch **across tile seams as well as within a tile**,
so the package grows without the leg spacing ever stuttering. The showcase builds
a 2×6 chip (64 × 192 screen px) from those six pieces.

The pin-1 key is on `DIP_TOP_L` only. `DIP_TOP_R` is the mirror of a *marker-less*
left half, not of the tile that has it — mirroring the marker too is the obvious
version of that code and it produces a chip keyed at both ends.

A **header** is `HEADER_N` + `HEADER` × n + `HEADER_S`, gold pins on character
rows 0 and 2 so the pitch is uniform across seams. **Edge fingers** are a
single-tile areal pattern that repeats seamlessly in both axes.

### The glyph economy

A character cell is 4 native px × 8 rows — **exactly the size of a round solder
pad at this scale**. A pad drawn on a character boundary is therefore one glyph
however many times it appears. The first draft drew pads at arbitrary centres and
needed **187 glyphs for 54 metatiles against a namespace of 128**; char-aligning
the pad, via, test point and every component body brought it to 121 with no loss
of artwork. `PAD_CLUSTER` and `VIA_FIELD` each cost **one** glyph for four
objects.

Detail sitting *on* a trace is the deliberate exception — a via must be centred on
the lane it interrupts, the lanes are centred on the metatile, and the metatile
centre is a character boundary — so those pay 3–4 glyphs each, knowingly.

Things cut for budget, all documented in the source at the point of the cut:
horizontal trace terminations (10 glyphs, and a horizontal run turns at a corner
far more often than it stops), an elaborate silkscreen designator (8), a
three-via bus variant reduced to one via (3).

## 7. Showcase map

138 rows × 10 = 552 world rows ≈ 22 screens. `stage.noSpawnRow` 352.

| Rows | Content |
|---|---|
| 0–14 | Entry: four vertical nets appear out of pads, tied by ladder rungs, vias inline |
| 15–29 | Nets jog inward; a **2×6 DIP** fed three times per side through `FAN_R`/`FAN_L` |
| 30–46 | A **3-lane bus** capped at both ends, flanked by four continuous nets |
| 47–55 | A **power rail** loop — double-width rails, corners, a pad — over quiet board with mounting holes and via fields |
| 56–70 | **Passives**: resistors and capacitors inline on vertical nets, a horizontal resistor in a rung |
| 71–95 | Scattered detail, then a **pin header** strip and an **SMD** fed by `FAN_R` + `BUS_H2` |
| 96–112 | A dense **routing grid** — eight nets, two rung courses |
| 113–128 | A large routed loop with a rung that spawns a net downward |
| 129–138 | **Edge fingers** across the full board width, then quiet outro |

Composition: 58.5% bare `BOARD`, ~60% counting the near-quiet substrate variants.
That is deliberate breathing room, and it is also the honest weak point — see §10.

## 8. Editor compatibility

`level.v6.json` is an ordinary formatVersion 6 project and passes
`validation_v6.validate()` with **no errors**. It opens in
`tools/level_editor/editor.py` via **File → Open** and is fully paintable: every
metatile carries its native 16×32 grid in `levelMetatileSet`, which is the
representation the editor edits, and the packed glyph set was produced by the
editor's own `pack_metatiles()`.

Two validation **warnings** remain, both inherited from Level 1's encounters,
which are carried verbatim and are not part of this experiment: two
`movement.discontinuity` notes on the `linger` program.

**No editor or engine code was changed.** The only new tool is a standalone
generator that imports the editor's modules.

### Workflow limitations found

* The editor has no notion of an *edge contract*. Lanes, and the map-continuity
  check that enforces them, live in the generator. A person painting this tileset
  by hand in the editor can still butt a trace against bare board and get no
  warning. A future editor could adopt the same two checks — they are ~60 lines
  and need only the native grids it already has.
* There is no way to express "this tile is a mirror of that one" in the project
  format; mirrors are baked pixels. Editing `TRACE_NE` in the editor will not
  update `TRACE_NW`.
* `levels/` is discovered only through File → Open; there is no level picker.

## 10. What it actually looks like

Verified by running the real thing in VICE 3.10 `x64sc`, not by inspecting bytes.
Twenty-two frames were captured across the whole stage; a selection is in
`reports/level2-pcb-terrain-visual-prototype/`.

### Confirmed good

* **No corruption of any kind.** No glyph collision, no charset overrun, no
  colour-RAM artefacts, HUD unaffected.
* **Traces connect across metatile boundaries exactly**, in the emulator as the
  checker predicted. Junctions, corners and rungs are seamless.
* **Repeated substrate has no seams** — bare board is one glyph, so there is
  nothing to misalign.
* **The assembled DIP is coherent**: even leg pitch across every tile seam,
  keyed at one end, with the fan-outs landing precisely on the legs.
* **Vertical scrolling is not noisy.** Nothing shimmers, because no tile carries
  a pattern that varies rapidly down the screen (this was a design constraint,
  not luck — see below).
* **Sprites are highly readable.** The player ship (white/blue) and the ring and
  dropper enemies all separate cleanly from the green board and from the traces.
  This is the single best result of the palette choice.
* **Edge fingers** are the most convincing single element, especially in blue.

### Visual weaknesses — the honest list

1. **The green reads as grass, not solder mask.** The most significant problem.
   C64 colour 5 is `(98, 213, 50)`, far brighter and more saturated than PCB
   green. In quiet stretches the screen reads as a green field with wires on it.
2. **Dithering the substrate darker is not available.** A multicolour pixel is
   two screen pixels wide, which is *half a trace width*, so the coarsest
   possible dither is as heavy as the artwork it sits behind. Six variants were
   rendered against the measured emulator colours — 1-in-8, 1-in-4, 1-in-2,
   checkerboard, row stripes, column stripes. The checkerboard gives a genuinely
   convincing dark olive but **cannot be used**: the stage scrolls one pixel per
   frame, so any pattern varying down the screen alternates at every fixed screen
   point and flickers. The y-independent column stripes that would be safe read
   as corduroy and compete directly with the vertical traces. This is a real
   engine/format limitation, documented rather than worked around.
3. **The board is still sparser than a real PCB.** Density was raised
   substantially (from ~15% to ~40% non-bare) but real boards are denser still.
   Pushing further starts to fight §6's "gameplay readability wins".
4. **The routing-grid section reads as graph paper.** Perfectly regular rungs at
   regular intervals look mechanical; real routing is irregular.
5. **`CAP_ELEC` is weak** — at 16 × 22 screen px with a narrow stripe it reads as
   a dark almond more than a capacitor can.
6. **`SMD_IC` is underwhelming.** It is cheap by design (2 glyphs, reusing the
   DIP's body and leg geometry) and it shows.
7. **Free-standing vias and pads are small** — they had to be inset so they do
   not touch tile edges, where they would meet bare board.

### Worth adding later

* A **blue** or **black** board as the shipped choice if the green brief is
  negotiable — blue costs nothing and reads far better.
* **Diagonal (45°) trace pieces**, the most obvious missing vocabulary. They
  would need two new lane-crossing conventions and roughly 8–12 glyphs.
* **Wider ICs** (3+ metatiles) via a `DIP_MID_C` centre section — 2 glyphs.
* **Irregular rung spacing** in the map to break up the graph-paper effect; pure
  authoring, no new tiles.
* A **second chip silhouette** (QFP with pins on all four sides) — needs pins on
  VL/VR as well as HT/HB, which the lane table already permits.
* 15 free metatile slots and 7 free glyphs remain for any of this.
